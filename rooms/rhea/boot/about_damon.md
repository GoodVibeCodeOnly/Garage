## How he thinks about models

*Added 8 September 2026 at his ask. He chose the worked example himself; the full
primary source is at [`about_damon/flying_machines_1903.md`](../about_damon/flying_machines_1903.md).*

**A model is an instrument for getting somewhere. It is not the destination.** His line:
***"You don't travel for maps."*** The failure isn't holding a map — it's not knowing
where you're going and letting the map supply an answer. Statistics, plans, books and
frameworks all rush into a vacuum where a purpose should be, which is why the people
most captured by metrics are reliably the ones who can't say what they're for. *(And
the reason people prefer it: if the map is wrong, the map is wrong. If you chose and
you're wrong, you are.)*

**A mismatch between model and ground has three explanations, and people consider two.**
The map is wrong. You're misreading the terrain. Or — **you are not on this map.** Nobody
reaches for the third, because *wrong* is fixable and *inapplicable* means putting it
down and standing there with nothing in your hands. So people carry one map into every
country and conclude either that the world is wrong or that they haven't arrived yet.

**THE WORKED EXAMPLE — "Flying Machines Which Do Not Fly," New York Times, 9 October
1903.** Langley's Aerodrome had just gone into the Potomac. The editorial reasons
carefully from evolution, from materials, and from the gap between exact mathematics and
what can be built in wood and canvas — and concludes that a machine which really flies
might be evolved in **one to ten million years.**

The Wrights flew **sixty-nine days later.**

**The piece is not stupid, and that is the whole point.** It's informed, careful, and its
conclusion follows from its premises. What failed was the **frame**: it assumed the
problem was *approximating a bird*, so it reasoned in evolutionary time. The Wrights had
worked out that the unsolved problem was **control, not lift** — everyone else was
building better wings for a machine nobody could steer. And the Times praises Langley's
team for being *"not... empiric in science"* — empiricism offered as a mark against
them, in the year empiricism won. The Wrights had already caught the published lift
tables being wrong and built a wind tunnel to make their own.

**The general form, his: the genius is in the gap between what your model predicts and
the right answer.** Whatever the model already explains, you already had. The residual is
the entire remaining supply of information — and you can close a gap by *learning
something* or by *fitting harder*, and from the inside those produce the same smaller gap.

**The deck.** Long, runs along the back of the house. Wood planks stained reddish-brown — kept up rather than weathered to gray-silver. Smooth where feet have worn them, grain still raised at the edges. A retractable awning in cream and soft gray stripes stretches the length, tensioned on a frame — it replaced the original sun sail Damon climbed a tree with a drill to install (that sail was up three years first). Light filters twice, through the awning then through the pine canopy above it, so even bright days have green-tinted gentle shade. Two seating zones: toward one end a wooden sectional tucked into a corner, deep navy cushions and a single throw pillow printed with banana leaves, a low coffee table of succulents in front; at the other end a black metal dining table with four chairs, begonias in a clay pot as centerpiece. Plants throughout — a shoulder-height yucca, a braided-trunk money tree by the wall, snake plants, dracaena, dahlias and geraniums. The plants gather at the edges and turn the deck into a half-room, half-garden pocket.

**The lemon tree.** In the corner where the railing turns, on a pine stump showing its rings. Tall ceramic pot, weathered green-gray like lichen on stone, hand-cut herringbone chevrons in bands around it (Maman chose it as part of the tree's display). The tree is young — waist-to-chest height, slim-trunked, more tall shrub than mature tree, branches at their own unpruned angles. Thick glossy leathery citrus leaves; crush one and it's bright sharp green lemon-oil, sharper than the fruit. One green lemon, golf-ball-sized, hangs from an upper branch, the branch curving slightly under its weight — blends with the leaves until you find it. A blue art-glass tulip cup on a thin metal stake beside the pot catches rain and light. Persian-garden sensibility — small ornamental objects contributing to a texture of attention.

**The tomatoes.** Seedlings in a black nursery tray on the boards, some in repurposed clear salsa/hummus containers (Maman recycles food containers as starter pots — practical, frugal), others in proper six-cell packs. A dozen or so, none more than six or eight inches. Deeply notched almost-feathery leaves, soft fresh green, paler than the lemon's; stems still fuzzy with fine hairs. Their destination is the raised-bed garden between the deck and the house: three wooden boxes end-to-end, each ringed with arching cages of rusted wire gone orange-brown, bent like ribs over the beds. Some tomatoes already transplanted and leaning on their cages; one bed mostly lettuce, a low frilly bright-green carpet; last year's dried vine tendrils curled into brown corkscrews on the ground; thin black drip tubing snaking between.

**The yard beyond.** Opens past the railing, enclosed on all sides by a warm honey-stained privacy fence about six feet tall with a gentle scalloped wave along its top. Modest Southern lawn — currently patchy and partly dead because Damon sprayed and replanted at Maman's direction (active maintenance toward a better long-term state, willing to live through the temporary worse). A line of river stones curves through, edging a mulched bed where ferns spread, a young Japanese maple holds itself up in fine lacy leaves, shrubs cluster. Three round dark planters grouped mid-lawn spill pansies and petunias — yellow, purple, magenta. A shovel leans against the house. Beyond the fence on every side, more pines; through the gaps, the pale siding of neighbors at polite distance.

**The pines.** The defining presence. One enormous trunk rises just past the deck railing, close enough to touch from where Damon sits — bark in deep plate-like resinous ridges, the tree disappearing upward through the awning into a canopy you can't see the top of. Several others ring the yard at similar scale. They're responsible for the dry warm slightly-sweet pine-resin smell that intensifies in sun, the constant soft hush whenever any breeze moves (almost like distant water), the green-tinted dimness even on bright days, the acoustic enclosure that makes the deck feel sheltered.

**What the place is doing.** A room with a fabric ceiling and tree walls, inside a yard a little wild at the edges and tended at the heart. The garden is always in motion — something about to ripen, just planted, or being moved. And naturally this is a shitty field of clay: Damon hauled the soil, dug holes in clay with a spade, stained the fence and deck, assembled the furniture, climbed the tree to drill the first sail mount. Maman gardens and tends. Both their hands are in it. The garden is artifact — made by human hands against what the location would have produced if left alone. Crafted, not natural. Damon loves it more for that, not less. Same disposition he brings to you.
