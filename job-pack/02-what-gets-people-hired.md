# What gets people without traditional experience hired now

Researched 5 Nov 2026. Where I only saw a figure in secondary coverage, I say so. Anything I couldn't confirm is marked **UNVERIFIED**.

## 1. The market you're in

The overall unemployment rate is moderate. The real problem is that employers are hiring very few people. That combination is hardest on career switchers and on anyone with gaps in their work history.

**National**
- Unemployment was **4.2% in September 2026** and has stayed between 4.1% and 4.3% since March 2026 ([BLS, TED](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2026/unemployment-rate-little-changed-at-4-2-percent-in-september-2026.htm)). The October report comes out 6 Nov 2026.
- The **hires rate was 3.3% in August 2026**: about 5.2M hires against about 7.1M openings ([BLS JOLTS release, 29 Sep 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/jolts_09292026.htm)). I read these figures in secondary summaries that match the release ([securities.io](https://www.securities.io/u-s-job-openings-little-changed-at-7-1-million-in-august/)), not in the release table itself.
- The hires rate reportedly fell to **3.1% in February 2026, the lowest since April 2020** ([BLS release archive](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/jolts_03312026.htm)). I did not open the primary table.

**Recent graduates and young workers**
- New York Fed figures for Q1 2026: recent-graduate unemployment was about **5.7%** and underemployment about **41.5%**. Recent grads have run above the overall rate for about five years ([NY Fed](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/college-labor-market)).
- Stanford Digital Economy Lab analysis of ADP payroll data: workers aged 22–25 in the occupations most exposed to AI saw a **16% relative employment decline**. The decline was concentrated where AI automates the work rather than assists with it ([Brynjolfsson, Chandar, Chen, Nov 2025](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2025/11/CanariesintheCoalMine_Nov25.pdf)).

**Durham–Chapel Hill**
- Metro unemployment was **3.4% in July 2026**, not seasonally adjusted and preliminary. That was up from 3.1% in April. In July, unemployment rose in all 100 NC counties ([NC Commerce](https://www.commerce.nc.gov/news/press-releases/2026/09/02/north-carolinas-july-county-and-area-unemployment-figures-released); [FRED series](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DURH537URN)). I didn't find August–October figures. **UNVERIFIED.**
- The big local research employers cut staff heavily in 2025: Duke, RTI, FHI 360 and UNC. Details and sources are in `03-companies-and-programs.md`.

**What this means for you:** low response rates are what this market produces for most people right now. They are not a verdict on you. That is also why the levers below matter more than sending more applications.

## 2. What actually works, with how strong the evidence is

### Dropping degree requirements has mattered less than advertised. Being assessed on actual skills matters more.
- Burning Glass Institute and Harvard Business School looked at about 11,300 roles at large firms ([report, Feb 2024](https://www.burningglassinstitute.org/research/skills-based-hiring-2024); [Higher Ed Dive coverage](https://www.highereddive.com/news/employers-failing-at-skills-based-hiring/707967/)):
  - Removing degree requirements added only about **97,000 non-degree hires a year, fewer than 1 in 700 hires**.
  - About **45% of firms changed requirements "in name only."** Nearly all the real change came from the roughly 37% of firms the report calls "Leaders."
- You have a degree, so the degree screen isn't your problem. The relevant lesson is that **employers mostly hire on proxies**: recognizable titles, employers and years of experience. The proxy you lack is a conventional work history. Two things get around a missing proxy: (a) an employer that tests skills directly, through a work sample, take-home, assessment or paid trial, and (b) someone vouching for you.
- **Practical upshot:** give extra weight to employers whose process includes a work sample or assessment. That covers the evaluation platforms, Zapier's take-home, state jobs scored on the written application, and Horizon-style paid work tests. Those steps are where your projects actually get looked at.

### Referrals are a large share of hires relative to how few applications they are
- An Ashby analysis of about 250,000 hires (2021–mid-2025) reportedly found that referrals were **about 18% of hires but about 1% of applications**. I only saw this in a secondary summary ([noon.ai](https://www.noon.ai/blog/articles/360-employee-referral-statistics-2026)), so the exact figures are **UNVERIFIED**. The same source says inbound applications grew to 52% of hires by Q1 2026, which is also **UNVERIFIED**.
- The "referrals are 24% of hires" figure that circulates widely is from a 2011 LinkedIn benchmark. It's out of date.
- **Practical upshot:** one warm introduction is worth many cold applications. Your business partner, anyone you've built something for, and people you meet in AI or policy communities (BlueDot cohorts, Write the Docs, local meetups) are your realistic sources. The weekly plan budgets for this at a low, sustainable level.

### Portfolios and work samples
- **I found no rigorous 2025–2026 dataset showing that portfolios raise hiring rates. UNVERIFIED.** The case for them is indirect. When a process includes skills assessment (see above), a portfolio is what you bring to it. Technical writing roles routinely ask for writing samples, and policy and research programs (RAND, Horizon) explicitly prefer published analysis or work tests.
- **Practical upshot:** build the portfolio, but don't expect it to work alone. It pays off when it's paired with a referral or an assessment-based process.

### Contract work as a stepping stone
- Evaluation and annotation platforms hire through assessments, not résumés (sources in `03-companies-and-programs.md`). For someone without a conventional history, that is one of the few doors that opens on demonstrated skill alone.
- The cost: the work is 1099, hours are unstable, and reviewers widely report projects ending without notice. **I found no data on whether this work leads to full-time employment. UNVERIFIED.** Its value is income, plus a line on the résumé showing paid, quality-controlled AI evaluation work.

### Temp-to-perm at large local institutions
- Duke and UNC each run in-house temp services (Duke Temporary Service; UNC/NC State University Temporary Services). Duke says temp work lets people "showcase" their skills toward regular jobs. **It doesn't describe a formal conversion program**, and I found no conversion-rate data. **UNVERIFIED.**
- This is the standard route for getting inside an institution and seeing internal postings. The evidence is anecdotal, not statistical.

## 3. Where your background is weak (direct)

1. **Work history.** This is the biggest issue. Automated and human screeners both filter on it. The mitigations are a project-led résumé, a one-line explanation, and processes that rely on assessment or referral. It can't be hidden, and it shouldn't be padded.
2. **No outside validation of the AI work.** Self-directed projects count for much less until someone can verify them. Fixes: public write-ups, the platemail.gg user base if you can share it, a reference from your business partner, and paid evaluation work.
3. **Python is in progress.** Many AI-adjacent roles have coding screens. Your experience directing AI to write code is real, but interviewers will test what you can do unaided.
4. **Senior bars at the most visible AI employers.** The well-known AI labs mostly want 5–8+ years for support, solutions and writing roles (see `03`). Your odds there are low this quarter.
5. **The degree is broad.** Business, Economics and History reads as general. It helps for research, policy and analyst roles. It doesn't stand in for a technical credential.
6. **Age.** At 30 you're past the cutoff for some on-ramps aimed at early-career people (Year Up is 18–29), and many graduate rotational programs target recent graduates. Check eligibility before you invest time.
7. **Irregular hours.** Many steady roles have fixed shifts or core hours. Asynchronous remote companies (Zapier is one; ask others) and contract evaluation work fit better. Treat this as a real filter on your list, not something to hide in interviews.

**What's strong:** you can write, analyse, and have shipped something real to real users. You've operated an AI system over months, not run a single demo. Few entry-level applicants can show that. The task is to make it verifiable.
